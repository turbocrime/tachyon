# Recovery Risk in the Current MiMC-Spectrum Scheme

## Scope

This note describes the recovery risk in the current branch's nullifier derivation scheme. It focuses on passive recovery: whether an observer of published nullifiers can recover enough hidden derivation material to predict future nullifiers for the same note.

The current branch derives nullifiers through certified MiMC-p5 spectrum polynomials and a weighted hidden-coset query:

$$
\mathsf{nf}_d = \sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\rho_j^d\,T_j(c\gamma^d)
$$

Here:

- $d$ is the epoch offset from the note creation epoch;
- $T_j$ are hidden emitter spectrum polynomials;
- $c$ is a note-specific secret coset shift;
- $\gamma$ is the public generator for the nullifier query domain;
- $\rho_j$ are note-specific secret geometric weight bases;
- only $\mathsf{nf}_d$ is intended to become public.

The implementation parameters are

$$
N=4
\qquad
L=8192
\qquad
S=16384
$$

where $N$ is the number of emitters, $L$ is the committed spectrum length per emitter, and $S$ is the nullifier query-domain size.

## Construction summary

The note master key has six field elements:

$$
\mathsf{mk}=(\mathsf{mk}_0,\ldots,\mathsf{mk}_5)
$$

From this key, the wallet derives:

- an expanded key schedule $K$ of length $128$;
- $N=4$ per-emitter salts $\mathsf{salt}_j$;
- the query shift $c$;
- the weight bases $\rho_j$.

The expanded key schedule is generated by a 64-round MiMC-p5 expansion. Each emitter polynomial $T_j$ is then the interpolation of an 8192-round MiMC-p5 state sequence. In evaluation form,

$$
T_j(\omega^r)=s_{j,r}
$$

for $r\in\{0,\ldots,L-1\}$, where $\omega$ generates the order-$L$ commitment domain.

The first state is fixed by the per-emitter salt and the first expanded key:

$$
s_{j,0}=(\mathsf{salt}_j+k_0)^5
$$

The remaining states satisfy the recurrence

$$
s_{j,r+1}=(s_{j,r}+k_{r+1}+C_{r+1})^5
$$

with the expanded key schedule read cyclically and $C_r$ the pinned MiMC constants.

The proof system certifies that the committed emitter polynomials satisfy this recurrence against the committed expanded key schedule. The native nullifier query is then

$$
\mathsf{nf}_d=\sum_j\rho_j^d T_j(c\gamma^d)
$$

## Spectrum-level underdetermination argument

If the emitter spectra were modeled as arbitrary hidden polynomials of degree less than $L$, then the hidden amplitude count would be

$$
NL=4\cdot8192=32768
$$

The maximum number of offsets exposed by one note is

$$
S=16384
$$

Thus the sizing invariant is

$$
S<NL
$$

Under the idealized model where the hidden spectrum amplitudes are independent unknowns, published nullifiers give at most $S$ equations in $NL$ hidden amplitudes. This leaves a large nullspace and prevents information-theoretic recovery of all emitter values from final nullifiers alone.

A monomial expansion makes this explicit. Write

$$
T_j(X)=\sum_{e=0}^{L-1} t_{j,e}X^e
$$

Then

$$
\mathsf{nf}_d
=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\sum_{e=0}^{L-1}
(t_{j,e}c^e)(\rho_j\gamma^e)^d
$$

At this level, the unknown amplitudes are the $NL$ values $t_{j,e}c^e$, plus the $N$ bases $\rho_j$. Since $S$ is only half of $NL$, the design intentionally stays below the generic sequence-recovery threshold suggested by this exponential-sum representation.

## Why this is not an information-theoretic proof

The real $T_j$ are not sampled as arbitrary independent polynomials. They are generated from note-specific key material through Poseidon and MiMC-p5. Therefore the true entropy is not $NL$ independent field elements.

The real recovery question is computational:

$$
\text{Given many }\mathsf{nf}_d
\text{ can an attacker predict future }\mathsf{nf}_{d'}
$$

A successful attacker does not need to recover the original note secrets. Any of the following would be enough:

1. the note master key material;
2. the expanded key schedule plus the salts, shift, and weight bases;
3. the emitter polynomials $T_j$, plus $c$ and $\rho_j$;
4. any alternative compact representation computing

   $$
   d\mapsto\sum_j\rho_j^dT_j(c\gamma^d)
   $$

   on future offsets.

The current scheme therefore relies on a computational assumption: the MiMC-derived spectra should be hard to recover or predict from weighted hidden-coset samples.

## Main recovery risks

### Risk 1: algebraic attacks on MiMC-derived spectra

The public sequence is not a generic PRF interface. It exposes many samples of a structured algebraic map:

$$
y_d=\sum_j\rho_j^dT_j(c\gamma^d)
$$

The emitter polynomials are constrained by a MiMC-p5 recurrence:

$$
s_{r+1}=(s_r+k_{r+1}+C_{r+1})^5
$$

A recovery attack could try to use this recurrence, the cyclic key schedule, and many off-domain evaluations to derive equations on the expanded key material. The public outputs are mixtures rather than direct trace states, which helps, but the construction should still be viewed as a concrete algebraic cryptographic assumption rather than an information-theoretic guarantee.

### Risk 2: the hidden shift and weights are part of the security boundary

The query parameters $c$ and $\rho_j$ help hide which spectrum points and which linear combination are being sampled. They must remain note-bound and unavailable to the attacker.

If $c$ were known, the attacker would know the exact evaluation point for every observation. If $\rho_j$ were known or chosen by an attacker, the sequence would become a more explicit linear system in the emitter evaluations or coefficients.

### Risk 3: the $NL$ count is not key entropy

The $NL=32768$ amplitude count is useful for sizing the query domain, but it should not be interpreted as secret entropy. The note starts from much smaller cryptographic key material and expands it.

Thus the current scheme's safety depends on Poseidon and MiMC-p5 acting as cryptographic expansion and mixing, not on the spectra being literally sampled as $32768$ independent field elements.

### Risk 4: proof soundness is separate from secrecy

The proof system proves that a wallet's witnessed polynomials are correctly generated and queried. It does not itself make those values hard to recover from public outputs.

Soundness-critical requirements include:

- the expanded key polynomial must match the committed expanded keyset;
- the emitter polynomials must satisfy the MiMC-p5 recurrence against the committed key schedule and public constants;
- the per-emitter boundaries must be fixed by the note-derived salts and first expanded key;
- the query shift and weight bases must be derived from note-bound material, not freely witnessed;
- the spend offset must be pinned by lineage continuity and consensus state.

A soundness failure here would be a correctness break rather than merely a passive recovery weakness. This note assumes those proof obligations are correctly enforced and focuses on passive recovery from the final nullifier sequence.

## What appears stronger than Alex's scheme

The current scheme avoids the most direct low-dimensional recovery issue. A MiMC-p5 emitter spectrum is not intentionally specified by $O(\log L)$ product factors. Its values are produced by many rounds of nonlinear cryptographic mixing and then observed only through weighted hidden-coset queries.

The intended security posture is therefore:

$$
\text{many observed }\mathsf{nf}_d
\not\Rightarrow
\text{efficient prediction of future }\mathsf{nf}_{d'}
$$

This is a computational claim that deserves cryptanalytic review, but it is qualitatively different from a construction where the entire sequence has a closed-form description with about one hundred effective parameters.

## Assessment

The recovery risk in the current MiMC-spectrum scheme is residual and assumption-based. The scheme exposes a structured algebraic sequence, so it is not proven secure merely by counting hidden amplitudes. Its safety depends on the pseudorandomness and algebraic resistance of the concrete Poseidon/MiMC-derived expansion and on correct proof binding.

However, the current scheme does not obviously collapse to the $O(N\log L)$ parameter-recovery problem present in Alex's binary-product construction. At the spectrum level, the public observations are intentionally fewer than the hidden amplitudes, and the spectra are generated by cryptographic mixing rather than by a simple multiplicative coefficient variety.
