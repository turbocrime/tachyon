# Recovery Risk in Alex's Binary-Product Spectrum Scheme

## Scope

This note analyzes the strongest reasonable version of Alex's suggested construction as a replacement for the construction of the nullifier **spectra**. It does not assume that the binary-product polynomial is used directly as the public nullifier polynomial.

The strongest variant considered here keeps the current weighted hidden-coset query layer:

$$
\mathsf{nf}_d = \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} \rho_j^d\,T_j(c\gamma^d)
$$

but constructs each hidden spectrum polynomial as

$$
T_j(X) = \prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\left(a_{j,i}X^{2^i}+b_{j,i}\right)
$$

The analysis gives Alex's scheme the following favorable assumptions:

- each emitter $j$ has independently sampled factors;
- the coefficients of $T_j$ are not revealed;
- individual values $T_j(c\gamma^d)$ are not revealed;
- the shift $c$ and the weight bases $\rho_j$ remain hidden;
- the attacker sees only final published nullifiers $\mathsf{nf}_d$, usually one or two per spend.

This is the strongest variant because the binary product is used only as hidden spectrum-generation machinery behind the same query interface as the current branch.

## Effective form after substitution

At offset $d$, the query point is

$$
X_d = c\gamma^d
$$

One emitter evaluates to

$$
T_j(c\gamma^d)
= \prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\left(a_{j,i}(c\gamma^d)^{2^i}+b_{j,i}\right)
$$

Absorb the secret shift into the factor coefficients:

$$
A_{j,i}=a_{j,i}c^{2^i}
$$

Then

$$
T_j(c\gamma^d)
= \prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\left(A_{j,i}\gamma^{2^i d}+b_{j,i}\right)
$$

and the public sequence has the effective form

$$
\mathsf{nf}_d
= \sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\rho_j^d
  \prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\left(A_{j,i}\gamma^{2^i d}+b_{j,i}\right)
$$

For prediction, the attacker does not need to recover the original $a_{j,i}$ and $c$ separately. Recovering the effective parameters $A_{j,i}$, $b_{j,i}$, and $\rho_j$ is enough to compute future values of the same nullifier sequence.

## Parameter count

For spectrum length

$$
L=8192=2^{13}
$$

the binary product needs

$$
m=13
$$

factors, because

$$
\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}2^i = 2^m-1 = 8191
$$

Each emitter is therefore controlled by roughly

$$
2m+1
$$

effective field parameters:

$$
(A_{j,0},\ldots,A_{j,m-1}),\quad
(b_{j,0},\ldots,b_{j,m-1}),\quad
\rho_j
$$

With $N=4$ emitters and $m=13$, the entire final-output sequence is described by about

$$
N(2m+1)=4(27)=108
$$

effective parameters. If one insists on counting the original variables before absorbing the common shift $c$, the count is $4\cdot 26+4+1=109$, but that extra separation is not necessary for prediction.

This is the core recovery risk: a sequence intended to tolerate thousands of public observations would be generated by only about one hundred effective field parameters.

Degenerate cases, such as zero factors, do not improve this situation. They reduce the effective dimension further or move the construction into an even more special algebraic subfamily.

## Coefficient structure

Even though the strongest variant does not reveal coefficients, the coefficient structure explains why the construction is algebraically fragile.

For one emitter,

$$
T(X)=\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}(a_iX^{2^i}+b_i)
$$

Every exponent $e\in\{0,\ldots,2^m-1\}$ has a unique binary representation. Let $S(e)$ be the set of bit positions equal to $1$. Then the coefficient of $X^e$ is

$$
C_e
= \prod_{i\in S(e)}a_i\prod_{i\notin S(e)}b_i
$$

If all $b_i\neq 0$, define

$$
B=\prod_i b_i
\qquad
r_i=\frac{a_i}{b_i}
$$

Then

$$
C_e=B\prod_{i\in S(e)}r_i
$$

So all $2^m$ coefficients lie on a low-dimensional multiplicative variety. For subsets $S,T\subseteq\{0,
\ldots,m-1\}$, they satisfy identities such as

$$
C_S C_T = C_{S\cap T} C_{S\cup T}
$$

In particular, if coefficients were revealed, then

$$
C_\emptyset
C_{\{0\}}
\ldots
C_{\{m-1\}}
$$

would determine every other coefficient. This is not the strongest attack model, but it shows that the product construction is not generating independent-looking spectrum amplitudes.

## Exponential-sum view

Expanding the product after substituting $X=c\gamma^d$ gives a second useful view:

$$
T_j(c\gamma^d)
= \sum_{e=0}^{L-1} C'_{j,e}\gamma^{ed}
$$

where the coefficients $C'_{j,e}$ are still constrained by the binary-product identities. Therefore

$$
\mathsf{nf}_d
= \sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\sum_{e=0}^{L-1}
C'_{j,e}(\rho_j\gamma^e)^d
$$

The current query layer turns every spectrum into an exponential sum over bases $\rho_j\gamma^e$. Alex's construction does not merely choose arbitrary coefficients $C'_{j,e}$; it restricts them to a compact product-parameterized family.

Thus the attack surface is not just that the sequence has algebraic structure. The current query layer already has algebraic structure. The additional problem is that Alex's proposed generator collapses the hidden coefficient family from $O(NL)$ apparent amplitudes to $O(N\log L)$ effective parameters.

## Observed-output recovery attack

Under the strongest hidden-spectrum variant, the attacker observes equations

$$
y_d = \mathsf{nf}_d
= \sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\rho_j^d
  \prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\left(A_{j,i}\gamma^{2^i d}+b_{j,i}\right)
$$

for many offsets $d$.

Each observed nullifier gives one equation in the effective unknowns. Once the number of observed nullifiers exceeds roughly

$$
N(2m+1)
$$

the system is generically overdetermined. With the branch's parameters, this threshold is about

$$
108
$$

observations.

A conceptual recovery strategy is:

1. collect observed pairs $(d,y_d)$;
2. write the equations

   $$
   y_d-\sum_j\rho_j^d
   \prod_i\left(A_{j,i}\gamma^{2^i d}+b_{j,i}\right)=0
   $$

3. solve the structured algebraic system for the effective parameters;
4. use additional observations to reject spurious solutions;
5. predict future nullifiers with the recovered effective representation.

This is not a proof of a practical break at exactly $108$ observations. The exact cost depends on the field, the solver strategy, the observed offsets, and implementation details. The risk is structural: the construction hands the attacker a small, rigid, closed-form algebraic target rather than a cryptographic expansion that is intended to behave pseudorandomly.

## Stronger leakage cases

The strongest variant assumes only final nullifiers are public. Any additional leakage makes recovery easier.

If individual emitter evaluations $T_j(c\gamma^d)$ become available, each emitter can be attacked separately. If enough coefficients of $T_j$ become available, the multiplicative coefficient identities recover the remaining coefficients. If $N-1$ emitter contributions are isolated, the final emitter contribution is obtained by subtraction from the public sum.

Similarly, if all but one factor of an emitter are known, then for any point $x$ where the known product is nonzero,

$$
\frac{T(x)}{\prod_{i\neq \ell}(a_i x^{2^i}+b_i)}
= a_\ell x^{2^\ell}+b_\ell
$$

Two evaluations at points with distinct $x^{2^\ell}$ determine $a_\ell$ and $b_\ell$.

## Assessment

Alex's binary-product spectrum construction is attractive because it is easy to constrain: each factor is simple and the binary exponents cover all coefficient slots exactly once.

That same simplicity is the recovery risk. The generated spectra have only $O(N\log L)$ effective degrees of freedom, not $O(NL)$. With $N=4$ and $L=8192$, this means about $108$ effective field parameters rather than thousands of independent-looking spectrum amplitudes.

Even in its strongest hidden-spectrum form, the construction makes published nullifiers samples from a low-dimensional algebraic family. It should not be treated as a safe replacement for a cryptographic expansion mechanism unless a concrete hardness argument is supplied for recovering or predicting

$$
d\mapsto \sum_j\rho_j^d
\prod_i\left(A_{j,i}\gamma^{2^i d}+b_{j,i}\right)
$$

from many observed outputs.
