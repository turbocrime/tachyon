# Comparison of Alex's Binary-Product Spectrum Scheme and the Current MiMC-Spectrum Scheme

## Scope

This note compares two ways to construct hidden nullifier spectra behind the same high-level query interface:

$$
\mathsf{nf}_d=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\rho_j^dT_j(c\gamma^d)
$$

The schemes differ in how the spectrum polynomials $T_j$ are generated.

Alex's strongest variant uses binary-product spectra:

$$
T_j(X)=\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}(a_{j,i}X^{2^i}+b_{j,i})
$$

The current branch uses MiMC-p5 spectra:

$$
T_j(\omega^r)=s_{j,r}
\qquad
s_{j,r+1}=(s_{j,r}+k_{r+1}+C_{r+1})^5
$$

The comparison focuses on recovery risk: whether published nullifiers allow an attacker to recover enough hidden structure to predict future nullifiers for the same note.

## Shared query layer

Both schemes can be placed behind the same final query:

$$
y_d=\mathsf{nf}_d=\sum_j\rho_j^dT_j(c\gamma^d)
$$

This layer provides the same basic hiding structure in both cases:

- the attacker sees only the final sum, not the individual emitter contributions;
- the query point lies on a note-specific hidden coset $c\langle\gamma\rangle$;
- each emitter contribution is scaled by a note-specific hidden geometric sequence $\rho_j^d$.

The main difference is the algebraic family from which the hidden functions $T_j$ are drawn.

## Common exponential-sum representation

For any degree-$<L$ emitter polynomial,

$$
T_j(X)=\sum_{e=0}^{L-1}t_{j,e}X^e
$$

Substituting $X=c\gamma^d$ gives

$$
\mathsf{nf}_d
=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\sum_{e=0}^{L-1}
(t_{j,e}c^e)(\rho_j\gamma^e)^d
$$

So the final public sequence is an exponential sum over bases $\rho_j\gamma^e$. The current design sizes the domain so that the number of possible public offsets stays below the number of apparent hidden spectrum amplitudes.

The decisive question is therefore how much freedom the amplitudes $t_{j,e}$ actually have.

## Alex's scheme: low-dimensional product family

For Alex's scheme,

$$
T_j(X)=\prod_i(a_{j,i}X^{2^i}+b_{j,i})
$$

After substituting $X=c\gamma^d$, define

$$
A_{j,i}=a_{j,i}c^{2^i}
$$

Then

$$
y_d
=\sum_j\rho_j^d
\prod_i\left(A_{j,i}\gamma^{2^i d}+b_{j,i}\right)
$$

For $L=8192=2^{13}$, one needs $m=13$ factors. Each emitter is controlled by about

$$
2m+1=27
$$

effective parameters, including its weight base $\rho_j$. With $N=4$, the full final-output sequence is controlled by about

$$
4(2\cdot13+1)=108
$$

effective parameters. Counting the original common shift separately gives $109$, but the absorbed $108$-parameter representation is enough for prediction.

The coefficient restriction is explicit. For one emitter, the coefficient of $X^e$ is

$$
C_e=\prod_{i\in S(e)}a_i\prod_{i\notin S(e)}b_i
$$

Equivalently, when all $b_i\neq0$,

$$
C_e=B\prod_{i\in S(e)}r_i
\qquad
B=\prod_i b_i
\qquad
r_i=\frac{a_i}{b_i}
$$

Thus the coefficients satisfy multiplicative identities such as

$$
C_SC_T=C_{S\cap T}C_{S\cup T}
$$

The recovery concern is structural: after roughly the effective-parameter count in observations, the attacker has an overdetermined algebraic system

$$
y_d-\sum_j\rho_j^d
\prod_i\left(A_{j,i}\gamma^{2^i d}+b_{j,i}\right)=0
$$

This is not a proof that a practical attack works at exactly $108$ observations. It is a warning that the proposed spectrum generator creates a compact closed-form model for the entire sequence.

## Current scheme: cryptographic expansion family

For the current MiMC-spectrum scheme, each emitter is generated by a long nonlinear recurrence:

$$
s_{j,r+1}=(s_{j,r}+k_{r+1}+C_{r+1})^5
\qquad
T_j(\omega^r)=s_{j,r}
$$

The branch parameters give

$$
N=4
\qquad
L=8192
\qquad
S=16384
$$

The idealized hidden-amplitude count is

$$
NL=4\cdot8192=32768
$$

while the maximum query domain is

$$
S=16384
$$

So the intended sizing margin is

$$
S<NL
$$

This does not prove security, because the MiMC-derived spectra are not independent random amplitudes. They are generated from smaller note-specific key material. The current scheme relies on a computational assumption: Poseidon and MiMC-p5 expansion should make the spectra hard to recover or predict from weighted hidden-coset samples.

The important contrast is that the current scheme does not intentionally impose the binary-product coefficient identities. Its trace values satisfy the MiMC recurrence on the interpolation domain, but that recurrence is the cryptographic mixing relation, not a simple low-dimensional multiplicative parameterization of all spectrum coefficients.

## Main contrast

Alex's scheme reduces spectrum generation to a compact product:

$$
T_j(X)=\prod_i(a_{j,i}X^{2^i}+b_{j,i})
$$

The current scheme uses iterative cryptographic mixing:

$$
s_{r+1}=(s_r+k_{r+1}+C_{r+1})^5
$$

The resulting recovery profiles differ.

For Alex's scheme:

- proving the construction arithmetically is likely easier;
- the effective parameter count is about $O(N\log L)$;
- coefficients satisfy simple multiplicative identities;
- final nullifiers are samples from a small algebraic family;
- recovery risk appears structural, not just a matter of parameter tuning.

For the current scheme:

- proving the construction is more expensive;
- the idealized spectrum-level amplitude count is $O(NL)$;
- security is computational, not information-theoretic;
- recovery depends on cryptanalysis of the concrete Poseidon/MiMC-derived construction;
- it does not obviously give the attacker a compact product-form representation of the sequence.

## Observation threshold intuition

Alex's strongest variant has an apparent nonlinear-parameter recovery threshold on the order of

$$
N(2\log_2 L+1)
$$

For $N=4$ and $L=8192$:

$$
N(2\log_2 L+1)=4(2\cdot13+1)=108
$$

The current scheme's spectrum-level sizing intuition is instead based on

$$
NL-S=32768-16384=16384
$$

These quantities are not security bits and should not be compared as exact attack costs. They measure different kinds of structure. Alex's number reflects a compact parametric model for the sequence. The current number reflects the gap between possible public samples and apparent hidden spectrum amplitudes.

## Soundness versus recovery

Both schemes also need proof soundness. The proof must ensure that the committed spectra being queried are actually generated by the claimed construction.

For Alex's scheme, the product relation might be cheaper to prove. But easier proof enforcement does not imply recovery resistance. A construction can be easy to constrain and still produce a sequence that is easy to model algebraically.

For the current scheme, the proof is more complex because it must bind:

- the expanded key polynomial;
- the MiMC-p5 recurrence;
- the emitter salts and first-state boundaries;
- the query shift and weight bases;
- the spend offset and adjacent nullifier pair.

A proof-soundness bug in either design would be a separate correctness failure. This comparison assumes the proof faithfully enforces whichever spectrum-generation rule is chosen.

## Bottom line

Alex's strongest variant is attractive as an arithmetic construction, but it appears unsafe as a spectrum generator because it collapses the hidden spectrum family to roughly $O(N\log L)$ effective parameters. Published nullifiers then become samples from a small algebraic family:

$$
d\mapsto\sum_j\rho_j^d
\prod_i\left(A_{j,i}\gamma^{2^i d}+b_{j,i}\right)
$$

The current MiMC-spectrum scheme has residual risk and should not be described as information-theoretically proven. Its security rests on the assumption that the MiMC-derived spectra remain computationally hard to recover or predict from weighted hidden-coset queries.

Nevertheless, for recovery resistance, the current approach is preferable to Alex's binary-product approach:

$$
\text{Alex: low-dimensional algebraic recovery risk}
$$

versus

$$
\text{Current: cryptographic hardness assumption plus spectrum-level underdetermination margin.}
$$

The current scheme still deserves cryptanalytic review, but Alex's proposed spectrum construction should not be considered a safe replacement without a concrete hardness argument for recovery from many observed final nullifiers.
